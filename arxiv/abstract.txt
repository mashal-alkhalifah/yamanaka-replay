Finding the few transcription factors that drive a cell-fate conversion is expensive: every query is a wet-lab assay, and the 2006 screen for the reprogramming factors consumed a documented 61 under a fixed leave-one-out protocol. We show that this class of screen is an instance of stochastic threshold group testing, and replay it on an oracle calibrated to published assay figures. Five results follow. (1) Information-gain design recovers the hidden recipe in a median of 10 experiments against 61, cheaper in 300 of 300 paired replicates; the same inference with random pools needs 44, locating the gain in experiment selection, not inference. (2) An apparent scaling limit, a classical search overtaking the designer at 48 candidates, is a property of the proposals rather than the method: changing only which pools may be scored drops the growth exponent from n^1.6 to n^0.43 at 100% recovery, where the classical search falls to 83%. (3) Fixing the recipe cardinality fails symmetrically and silently, at 0.999 confidence whether the true recipe is smaller or larger; making size latent removes this for one extra experiment. (4) Audited against two measured perturbation datasets, the assumed gap response of 0.10 should be 0.77; re-run on that structure the designer as calibrated recovers 2.5% of recipes, at mean posterior confidence 0.991 on its failures. (5) That precondition is removable: making the gap response latent recovers 99.3%, at 0.4 experiments above an oracle told its value, and beats a five-experiment calibration pilot on cost and accuracy. The replay validates the procedure, not the oracle, which was calibrated from the screen replayed; only (4) tests it against independent data. Results 3-5 are one finding: every assay parameter such a designer fixes is a silent failure awaiting a system that disagrees, and the remedy is to make it latent rather than calibrate it better.
